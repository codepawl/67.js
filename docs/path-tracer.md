# Cook (path tracer)

**Cook** (`67js/addons/path-tracer`) is the 67js offline path tracer (a nod to both the Cook-Torrance microfacet BSDF and *"let it cook"*). Alongside `Glaze` (`Renderer67`) for real-time WebGL2, `Cook` draws one still by following light paths on the CPU or GPU. It loads only when a render job asks for it, so a normal scene pays nothing (issue #57).

```js
import { Cook, renderPathTracedImage } from '67js/addons/path-tracer';

const { rgba, linear, report } = await renderPathTracedImage(
  { parts, environment, camera },        // the same objects the rasterizer takes
  { width: 960, height: 540, samples: 64, bounces: 8, seed: 0, denoise: true, threads: 4, maxSeconds: 300, noiseThreshold: 0 },
);
```

From an agent, submit an `image` render job with `settings.renderer` (see [render jobs](agent-render-jobs.md#path-tracer)).

## What it draws

Unidirectional path tracing against the triangle BVH that the [light bake](light-bake.md) uses, with next-event estimation and the environment map drawn by importance, then a wavelet denoiser. The first hit of every path samples every light. Later hits draw one light at random and divide by its chance, so the average is the same and a scene with eleven lights costs one shadow ray per bounce instead of eleven.

- Surfaces: diffuse, GGX specular with metals, clear coat, glass (`transmission`, `ior`, rough glass), emission, image maps (`map`, with `alphaMode: "mask"` cutouts, `normalMap` with `normalScale`, and `metallicRoughnessMap`, whose green channel scales roughness and blue scales metallic as in the rasterizer; box-projected materials read all of them through the UVs of their projection, the same ones the glTF export writes) and vertex colour. Skinned parts are drawn at their current pose.
- Lights: the sun (`keyColor`, `lightDirection`, as a 1 degree disk), point lights with a radius (a sphere, as Blender draws it with Soft Falloff off; Blender's default Soft Falloff makes it an oriented disk, which Cook does not model, see [Cycles parity](cycles-parity.md#sphere-lights-and-soft-falloff-1040)), spot lights with a soft cone, rectangular area lights, and the environment map with its rotation. All of them cast soft shadows, whatever their `castShadow` says, as in Cycles. Light linking is honoured: a light with `affects` (or the sun with `environment.keyAffects`) adds nothing at a part it skips, as a direct light or as a bounce source, and still shadows the other lights.
- Faces on one plane: the trace uses the same cut geometry as the raster view (faces hidden under a face on their plane are removed), and a face the flush pass ranked in front of another instead of cutting it is lifted by 0.1 mm per rank, so the face in front is the one a ray hits (#751).
- Light that bounces between surfaces, and the sky seen in mirrors and through glass.
- Rough metal that keeps its light: the GGX lobe gives back the light that bounces between microfacets (Fdez-Aguera 2019, from a table of the lobe's albedo), and after a bounce that spread the light the glossy lobes are widened the way Cycles does with `filter_glossy` 1, so a floor lit through a steel surface does not turn into rare, very bright speckles (#959).
- Emission: `emission` times `emissiveColor` (the base colour when none is given) times the `emissiveMap` texel at the hit, so a screen or a sign glows with its picture and lights what is near it. An emissive surface is found by the paths that hit it, not sampled as a light, so a small bright panel is noisy at low sample counts.
- Volume fog: `environment.volumeFog` is traced as a homogeneous medium inside its box, with its `density`, `color` (the scattering albedo), `anisotropy` (Henyey-Greenstein) and `maxDistance`. The distance to each scattering event is drawn from the exact free-flight distribution, so there is no step count and no banding. Every event is lit by next-event estimation (sun, point, spot, area lights and the sky, with shadow rays) and light reaching a surface through the fog is dimmed by it. Fog events count as bounces. `noiseAmount`, `noiseScale`, `drift`, `steps` and the `ambient` glow are rasterizer tricks and are not used.

## Subsurface

A part with a material `subsurface` weight above 0 sends that share of its diffuse light through a bounded random walk, after the Random Walk model of Blender's Principled BSDF (issue #283). The walk starts inward from the hit with a cosine-weighted direction, scatters isotropically with a per-channel mean free path of radius times scale in metres (one channel draws the distance and the other two are weighted by the mean density, so every channel stays unbiased), and leaves where it meets the part's own surface; there the path continues as white diffuse light with the walk's throughput, lit by every light. The colour is the block's `color`, or the base colour, mapped to a single scattering albedo with the fit Cycles uses, so a colour of 1 gives an albedo of 1 and gains no energy. A walk ends after 32 scattering events (`SUBSURFACE_WALK_STEPS`) or when it meets another part, and then its path ends. A point with no surface of its own behind it is a card: half of the colour is reflected on the near side and half is transmitted through it. The specular lobe and clear coat stay as they are, and a weight of 0 takes the unchanged code path (the same samples and pixels). The GPU tracer traces the block too (`src/path-tracer-gpu-subsurface.js`): the walk is compiled into its kernel only for a scene with a part above weight 0, the block sits in its own table of two texels per material, and a scene without one compiles and draws the kernel it always did (foliage and glass drawn by the kernel before and after this change are byte identical). On the `subsurface` reference scene (960 by 540, 64 samples, FLIP against the Cycles render with subsurface on) the GPU tracer scores 0.0795 with weight 1 and 0.0876 with weight 0, the CPU tracer 0.0795 and 0.0875, and GPU against CPU is 0.0029 and 0.0014 (measured 2026-11-04). The kernel with the walk compiled in cold in 42.8 s against 7.2 s for the plain one, since a second place that traces rays is inlined again.

## What it does not draw

`report.ignored` lists the ones this scene asks for; a render job returns it as `metrics.pathTracer.ignored`. It names occlusion maps, anisotropy, iridescence, sheen, moss, splat layers, baked `lightMap`s, tagged materials (foliage, liquid, water and the like, traced as plain surfaces), blended transparency, and the fog fields above.

- The procedural sky when there is no environment map (the sky is black).
- Occlusion maps. Normal and metallic-roughness maps are drawn on UV-mapped and on box-projected materials. Material graphs and patterns are drawn through the colour map they bake.
- Translucent leaves without a `subsurface` block (foliage looks darker than in the rasterizer), liquids, foam and blended transparency (traced as plain or opaque surfaces).
- Lights seen in mirrors (area lights and spot lights are lit but not visible), caustics, motion blur, bloom and colour grade. Depth of field is drawn only by the native `cycles` kernel, for a camera with an `fStop` (a thin lens with Cycles' aperture, focus distance and blades; see the render-native README); the CPU, GPU and legacy native tracers draw a pinhole.
- Multiple-scattering GGX: rough metal is a little darker than in Blender.

## Limits

| Option | Default | Range | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `width`, `height` | 960, 540 | 16 to 4096 | Output size, at most 2,073,600 pixels. |
| `samples` | 64 | 1 to 1024 | Paths per pixel. Every pixel draws all of them unless `noiseThreshold` is above 0. |
| `bounces` | 8 | 1 to 16 | Reflections followed by each path. |
| `seed` | 0 | 0 to 2147483647 | Changes the noise. |
| `threads` | 4 | 1 to 8 | Module workers; the pixels are the same for any count. Without `Worker` it runs on one thread. |
| `noiseThreshold` | 0 | 0 to 1 (render jobs: 0 to 0.1) | 0 draws every sample. Above 0, a pixel stops once the standard error of its mean luminance, relative to that mean plus 0.05, is under this, checked from 16 samples on and after every 8 more. It costs quality on some scenes (see the table below). It reads only the pixel's own samples, so the result is the same on any thread count and band split. `report.averageSamples` says how many paths a pixel took. |
| `maxSeconds` | 300 | 1 to 1800 | Wall-clock limit. Passing it throws; there is never a partial image. |
| `denoise` | true | boolean, `"wavelet"`, `"oidn"`, `"none"` | `true` or `"wavelet"` filters the light with normal, depth and noise guides; `false` or `"none"` does not filter. `"oidn"` is [Open Image Denoise](#open-image-denoise-optional): a render job with it defaults to 16 samples. |
| `backend` | `cpu` | `cpu`, `gpu`, `webgpu` | `gpu` traces in a WebGL2 shader, `webgpu` in WGSL compute (see Choosing a backend). |
| `fireflyClamp` | 20 | 1 to 1000 | Largest radiance one bounce may add to a sample. |

Also checked before any ray is traced: samples times pixels times bounces at most 4 billion, 400,000 triangles (the CPU and WebGL tracers; a scene exported to the native tracer takes more, see below), 1,024 parts, 32 lights, and finite numbers and in-range indices in every part. A render job limits `maxSeconds` to 600: the job runs on workers without blocking the page, and its deadline is `maxSeconds` plus 60 seconds for the page and the PNG, so a trace that reaches its limit still ends before the job does.

### Instanced scenes for the native tracer

`buildTraceScene(parts, environment, { instancing: true })` keeps parts that share one geometry object as instances of it, and `buildNativeInputV2` packs them as one bottom-level structure per geometry plus one instance record per part, for `traceNative` with `kernel: "cycles"`. Without the option the scene is flattened as before, and a scene where no two parts share a geometry is flattened too, with output identical to the default.

- Sharing is decided after the trace geometry is resolved. A skinned, flush-lifted or box-mapped part gets a geometry of its own, and a geometry with per-triangle materials is not shared either: those parts are moved into world space once and traced as instances with an identity matrix.
- Limits: 400,000 triangles counted once per shared geometry, 1,024 distinct geometries (the part limit, counted on geometries) and 65,536 instances. `buildTraceScene(parts, environment, { instancing: true, maximumTriangles })` replaces the 400,000 for a scene exported to the native tracer: an integer from 1 to 4,000,000 (`MAX_NATIVE_TRACE_TRIANGLES`) bounds the unique triangles (all triangles of a flattened scene), and four times that bounds the triangles with every copy counted. A value outside that range is refused, and the CPU and WebGL tracers never pass it, so they keep 400,000 and their error text. A native render job passes 1,500,000 (see "Triangle bound of a native scene" in [cycles-parity.md](./cycles-parity.md)). An instanced scene has no world-space triangle arrays or CPU BVH, so only `buildNativeInputV2` reads it.
- A part's material is its own entry in `materials`, so copies may differ in material; a multi-material mesh stays one geometry with the materials the part names.
- `buildNativeInput` (the `legacy` kernel) refuses an instanced scene with an error; build the scene without `instancing` for that kernel. `traceNative` with `kernel: "legacy"` refuses a FORMAT-v2 input the same way.
- Render jobs with `renderer.backend: "native"` keep repeated geometry as instances when the job traces with the `cycles` kernel (the default): the job page exports FORMAT-v2 tables of the unique triangles and one record per part, the job runner rebuilds the input for `traceNative`, and the report names the `instances` and counts `triangles` once per shared geometry. With `JS67_NATIVE_KERNEL=legacy`, `JS67_NATIVE_INSTANCING=off` (flattened triangles on the `cycles` kernel, to compare the two), or a scene where no geometry is shared, the job exports flattened geometry as before; on the `cycles` kernel the materials are still sent whole (next point), and only the legacy kernel reads the v1 tables.
- A job sends the materials of an instanced scene and of a flattened one the same way (`buildJobNativeInput` and `buildFlattenedJobNativeInput` in `packages/render-native/job-input.mjs`): what the v1 material tables carry is read from them, and `carriedMaterialFields` lays blend opacity, sheen, specular, anisotropy, iridescence, attenuation and the layer textures over it. A part looks the same whether or not other geometry in the scene is shared, and a material that sets none of those fields packs to the record the v1 tables gave. Every job on the `cycles` kernel is exported as FORMAT-v2; the legacy kernel and light bakes keep the v1 tables. A job's maps go from the materials into the FORMAT-v2 texture pool without the v1 atlas (`withVirtualAtlas` in `packages/render-native/pack-input.mjs`), so the pool (`TEXTURE_POOL_MAX_BYTES`, 1 GiB, mip levels counted) limits them and not the 4096 by 16384 texels of the atlas; a scene that fitted the atlas packs to the same bytes (`tests/native-job-texture-pool.test.js` holds the SHA-256 of every table). Over the pool the job throws a `TexturePoolFullError` (a `RangeError`, `texturePoolFull: true`) that says "The texture pool is full" and to pass smaller textures; the page export (`exportNativeSceneTables`) rethrows it and does not fall back to flattened or v1 tables, which would only fail on the atlas. A map is checked against the pool and against its data length (`width * height * 4` bytes) before it is read. The packers take `texturePoolMaxBytes` to lower the pool limit for a run (never to raise it). When the v1 fallback fails too, its error ends with the reason the FORMAT-v2 export failed first.
- Texture mip levels are opt in on the `cycles` kernel: pass `textureMips: true` to the packer (or `mips: true` on one texture) and every linearly filtered texture, RGBA8, sRGB and float, stores a mip chain after level 0 (level 1 integrates the level 0 reconstruction, the rest are 2 x 2 boxes); the kernel then keeps a ray cone per path and reads the level a hit asks for, blended between two levels. Blender Cycles reads level 0 only, so the default stays level 0 and an existing scene packs and renders byte for byte as before. The pool check counts the levels (a third more texels). The estimate, its limits and the measured error of a minified checker are in `docs/cycles-parity.md` ("Texture mip levels").
- When the instanced packer refuses a scene (copies of an emissive mesh that emit different triangles, a singular matrix), the job exports it flattened as before and the report says `instancing: "flattened"` and `instancingReason`. A scene that fails to export no longer marks the native tracer unusable for the process; only a tracer or adapter failure does.
- A part with `shadowOnly: true` becomes an instance that camera rays skip (visibility bits for every other ray kind stay set). An instance whose material differs from the first copy's emits light by its own material: a copy made emissive by its material is sampled as a light, and a copy made non-emissive is not. Triangles of a shared mesh that are emissive keep one `local_emissive_offset` for all copies, so a copy scaled to a size that drops an emissive triangle the other copies keep is refused with its index.

A part whose matrix mirrors (negative determinant) is flattened with its vertex normals kept outward and the last two corners of each triangle swapped, so the cross-product normal of every triangle points outward too. The native `cycles` kernel derives its geometric normal from that winding, and an instanced mirrored part reaches the same result through its negative-scale flag; a scene without a mirrored part is unchanged byte for byte.

The same scene and options give the same bytes. Random numbers come from a hash of the pixel, the sample and the seed, never from `Math.random` or the clock, and a pixel does not depend on which thread drew it.

## Choosing a backend

`backend: "native"` is an agent render job setting, not a `renderPathTracedImage` option: [`packages/render-native`](../packages/render-native/README.md) runs the same kernel on Vulkan ray queries. On the demo room at 1920 by 1080 and 64 samples an MCP image job took 25 to 35 s with native against 58 s with gpu (trace alone 2.7 to 3.1 s against 38.8 s; the rest is the page export, the wavelet denoise and the PNG), FLIP 0.0047 between them. A missing package, binary or adapter falls back to gpu with a note in the job report. `maxSeconds` is honoured: past it the native trace stops and the job fails, as on the other backends, instead of rerunning on gpu; `noiseThreshold` is too (the kernel applies the CPU stop rule per pixel, so `averageSamples` falls below `samples`; the `gpu` and `webgpu` backends still draw every sample). Volume fog and subsurface scattering are in the native kernel, compiled in only for a scene that uses them; `bench/native-tracer/scenes.mjs` draws the reference scenes on native and on the WebGPU megakernel for a FLIP comparison. Alpha cutouts (`alphaMode: "mask"`) work: cutout triangles are their own geometry and the kernel confirms a hit only when the colour map alpha reaches the cutoff, with no step limit. In a dense cutout scene native is the more correct tracer: the CPU and WebGL2 shadow ray takes the first hit the traversal finds, not the nearest, and when that hit is a transparent texel it skips occluders it had not reached yet, so those backends leak light (300 leaf cards: native vs cpu FLIP 0.043, and 0.0002 once the CPU shadow ray takes the nearest hit; bench/native-tracer/cutout-bisect.mjs shows one card agrees exactly). The steps before: up to 8 layers deep (`bench/native-tracer/cutout.mjs`: 300 masked leaf cards trace in 0.1 s at 640 by 360 and 64 samples). See [agent-render-jobs.md](agent-render-jobs.md#path-tracer).

The native package has two kernels. `traceNative` keeps `legacy`, the kernel described above, as its own default, but a render job with `backend: "native"` (issue #970) traces with the `cycles` kernel and denoises with OIDN and the albedo and normal guides when `denoise` is `true` (the default); `JS67_NATIVE_KERNEL=legacy` makes the job runner use the legacy kernel again, and without OIDN installed (or when it fails) the GPU a-trous filter of the native tracer runs (`denoiser: "atrous"`, biased: it smooths) with a note in `denoiserNote`; `denoise: "wavelet"` picks the CPU filter (on `metal`, `lit-interior` and `metal-room` at 960 by 540, 64 and 256 samples, NVIDIA A10: the a-trous filter took 6 to 8 ms against 3.8 to 4.3 s for the wavelet filter and 0.23 s for OIDN, with the same relMSE as the wavelet filter to six digits and OIDN at 0.5 to 0.9 of it, #1018). The job report names the `kernel`, the `noiseThreshold` and the `averageSamples`. On the `metal` reference scene (960 by 540, 256 samples, NVIDIA A10, Vulkan, scored against Cycles 5.3 at 4096 samples with relMSE, the 100 worst pixels dropped, and FLIP): the default job frame step (`bench/native-tracer/metal-job.mjs`) took 1.64 s (1.22 s trace, 0.23 s OIDN) with relMSE 0.00007, against 5.57 s for the wavelet filter (relMSE 0.00014), 1.36 s for no filter (relMSE 0.00019, flat-area grain 0.0076 against 0.0024 filtered) and 1.23 s for the legacy kernel with OIDN (relMSE 0.00093, FLIP 0.039 against 0.009). `cycles` is the opt-in kernel that follows Blender Cycles 5.3: on six reference scenes at 4096 samples it matches Cycles within 1 percent of the mean and within 1.8 percent on every 8 by 8 tile, and its error at 256 samples on `lit-interior` is 0.0027 against 0.0198 for the legacy kernel (Cycles: 0.0031). Three options follow Cycles: `sampler` (`'sobol-burley'` blue-noise pattern by default, `'white'` for the hash sampler), `filterType` and `filterWidth` (`'box'` width 1 by default, `'gaussian'` and `'blackman-harris'` from the Cycles filter table) and `lightSampling` (`'auto'`, `'tree'` or `'uniform'`; the Cycles light tree is used from more than one emitter). It is 2.3 to 3.1 times slower per sample than the legacy kernel before the light tree (0.92 s against 2.86 s on `lit-interior`, 960 by 540, 256 samples), and the light tree adds 16 to 32 percent per sample on top; at equal error against Cycles 5.3 it is 1.2 times faster on `lit-interior` and 3 to 6 percent slower on `many-lights-500` on an RTX 3060, and 1.5 times faster and level (1.02 to 1.03 times) on an NVIDIA A10G. Tables, method and the equal-error caveat are in [cycles-parity.md](cycles-parity.md#selecting-the-kernel).

`backend: 'cpu'` (the default) is the reference: it reuses `src/triangle-bvh.js` and `src/ray-query.js`, runs on workers in the job page next to the scene, needs no GPU, and gives the same bytes for the same scene and options. It measured 25 to 238 seconds for a 960 by 540 still at 32 samples on a Ryzen 5 5600X with four threads, while another session also loaded the machine (table in `bench/reference/README.md`).

`backend: 'gpu'` (issue #302) runs the same algorithm in a WebGL2 fragment shader (`src/path-tracer-gpu.js`, loaded only for this backend, so a CPU render and the core bundle do not carry it). The BVH, triangles, materials, maps, lights and the environment table are packed into float textures (`src/path-tracer-gpu-pack.js`), the random numbers are the same integer hash and PCG stream as the CPU tracer and are drawn in the same order, and each sample adds into four float targets (colour and depth, albedo, normal, luminance and its square) that feed the same denoiser. Float32 maths and a 24-bit random value make the pixels differ a little from the CPU tracer: FLIP (LDR) of GPU against CPU at the same seed was 0.0001 to 0.0018 on the 16 path tracer reference scenes at 480 by 270 and 32 samples. It needs a page with WebGL2, `EXT_color_buffer_float` and `EXT_float_blend`, and throws without them, so choose it only where those exist. It traces everything the CPU tracer does (lights with linking, all materials and maps including emissive maps, glass, volume fog, the sky); it does not use `noiseThreshold` (every pixel draws every sample) or `threads`, and a sample that produced a NaN or an infinity counts as black. The first still of a page compiles the kernel (13 to 30 s), later ones reuse it.

The 1920 by 1080 still of the demo room at 64 samples took 58.15 s on the GPU (RTX 3060, clean image, including 10.3 s of compiling with a warm shader cache, the BVH build and the denoiser; a cold compile measured 30 s on an earlier version of the kernel, which would have put it over 60 s; remeasured 2026-11-04 below) against 585.9 s on the CPU tracer (measured 2026-11-03; remeasured 2026-11-04 at 38.7 s for a first compile of a kernel text the driver had not seen, 7.0 s of it compiling, and 41.0 s for the same kernel again, 7.3 s compiling, each in a fresh headless Chrome profile, so the 60 s bound holds cold: the compile no longer differs between cold and warm on this machine, and `gpuCompileSeconds` in the job report records it; a scene with a subsurface part compiles a larger kernel, 42.8 s cold in the same setup, which adds that to its still; see [the WebGPU decision](webgpu-decision.md) for the choice of WebGL2 over WebGPU). Against Cycles (FLIP, 960 by 540, 64 samples, 16 scenes, `bench/reference/path-tracer.json` for the CPU and `path-tracer-gpu.json` for the GPU), the two backends scored within 0.0002 of each other on every scene (largest gap 0.0001 to 0.0002 on `foliage`, `glass`, `character`), so each keeps its score within the 0.005 bound (measured 2026-11-04). The GPU run took 4 to 13 s per scene after the first scene's compile. `node bench/reference/gpu-compare.mjs <directory> <scene or recipe.json> --backend both` draws a scene with both backends, and `bench/reference/flip-compare.py` scores them against each other and against Cycles. A render job selects it with `settings.renderer.backend: "gpu"` (see `docs/agent-render-jobs.md`); the job default stays `cpu`.

`backend: 'webgpu'` (issue #625) runs the same algorithm as WGSL compute (`src/path-tracer-webgpu.js` and `src/path-tracer-webgpu-kernel.js`, loaded only for this backend). It takes the same packed scene tables as storage buffers, draws the same random numbers in the same order and returns the same buffers, so the denoiser and tone mapping are unchanged. It needs `navigator.gpu` and an adapter with 10 storage buffers per shader stage; without them it throws an error that names `gpu` and `cpu` (the MCP `cpu` runtime has no WebGPU). Volume fog and subsurface scattering are traced too (`src/path-tracer-webgpu-medium.js`, the WGSL form of the WebGL2 pieces with the same random numbers): they are compiled into the megakernel only for a scene that uses them, and the subsurface table rides in the materials buffer, so a scene without them compiles the kernel it always did. The wavefront kernel does not trace them and throws a `RangeError` that names the megakernel. The same kernel starts paths at lightmap texels for the light bake (`bakeLightmapsAsync(..., { backend: 'webgpu' })`, see `docs/light-bake.md`). Measured on the RTX 3060 (2026-11-09, `docs/webgpu-decision.md`): the kernel compiles in 0.5 s cold against 29 s for the WebGL2 kernel, the demo room at 1920 by 1080 and 64 samples traces in 8.0 to 8.5 s against 15.2 s (three alternating runs), and FLIP against the WebGL2 backend is 0.0000 to 0.0019 on 15 of the 16 reference scenes (the sixteenth, `fog`, was not supported then; with fog and subsurface ported, 480 by 270 at 32 samples without the denoiser, RTX 3060, one run on a loaded machine, 2026-11-09: `fog` 0.0000 FLIP against WebGL2 in 1.35 s against 33.8 s, `subsurface` 0.0039 in 2.28 s against 174 s; the WebGL2 numbers are mostly the 17 and 106 s kernel compile, which is 1.0 and 1.9 s on WebGPU). The default kernel is a megakernel; a wavefront kernel (generate, extend, shade, shadow, with optional sorting by material) is built and was slower on this scene (17.3 to 18.1 s), so it is opt-in through `traceOnWebGpu` only. The default backend stays `cpu`.

`lit-interior` scored 0.1993 at 32 samples before adaptive sampling; it was not re-run with every sample at 64. A render job passes the value as `settings.renderer.noiseThreshold`, 0 to 0.1.

Fewer samples with the denoiser lose little on some scenes: 24 samples scored within 0.005 of 64 on `brick-graph` (0.1394 against 0.1370) and `character` (0.1164 against 0.1116), at about 45 percent of the time. Two scenes are not proof for all. Drawing one light at the first hit halves the time but makes the image noisier than 24 samples cost, so it was not kept.

The 1920 by 1080 still of `tests/fixtures/demo-room.recipe.json` at `samples: 64`, through an MCP image job with 8 threads: 246.4 s in the tracer (229.4 s tracing, the rest the filter), 30.7 samples per pixel on average, 257 s for the whole job (measured, once, with `noiseThreshold` 0.03, the setting at that time). With the default of 0 every pixel draws all 64, so the same still takes 585.9 s in the tracer and 596.1 s for the job (measured 2026-11-01 on 8 threads, machine at 100 percent CPU), inside the 600 s limit with almost no room: lower `samples` or pass a `noiseThreshold` for a larger room. That is 6 seconds over the old 240 s job limit, so the limit is now 600. The hair strands were not the cost by themselves: the time was per ray, and every ray was slow.

## Open Image Denoise (optional)

`settings.renderer.denoise: "oidn"` filters the still with [Intel Open Image Denoise](https://github.com/RenderKit/oidn) 2.5.1 (Apache-2.0), the denoiser Cycles uses, fed the colour, albedo and normal images the tracer writes. It is the package `@codepawlhq/67js-denoise-oidn` (`packages/denoise-oidn`), not part of the core archive: install it beside `67js` and run its `fetch` script, which downloads the official release binary for the platform after a SHA-256 check. The job runner calls the `oidnDenoise` tool on the CPU. A source checkout also finds the package in `packages/denoise-oidn`. On the native backend `denoise: true` means OIDN when it is installed. Without the package or the binary the job runs the wavelet filter and says why in `metrics.pathTracer.denoiserNote`; `metrics.pathTracer.denoiser` always names the filter that ran. Only the native build is shipped. The browser port (`denoiser` 0.0.11 on tensorflow.js) is a 134 MB install; its scores are not in the table because the GPU it needs was not free for this comparison (`bench/reference/denoise-browser.mjs` is the script to run it), and the job runner already has Node.

Compared on 960 by 540 captures of six reference scenes, FLIP against Cycles, lower is better (`bench/reference/denoise.json`, made by `denoise-capture.mjs`, `denoise-compare.mjs` and `denoise-score.py`). ## Progressive preview, blue noise and light choice (issue #623)

`67js/addons/path-tracer-progressive` (`createProgressiveTracer(scene, options)`) draws the GPU tracer one sample per pixel per `step()`. The first two steps draw at a quarter and a half of the size, one sample each, then every step adds a sample at full size; `update({ camera, parts, environment })` starts the image over, and `snapshot()` reads the image back (filtered unless `denoise` is off). It shares the WebGL2 context of the GPU still, so one render at a time owns it. The core bundle does not carry it.

Three options (`samplingPattern: "blue-noise-first"` and `lightSelection: "importance"` need the GPU backend, `lightSelection: "tree"` runs on the CPU reference, the WebGL2 GPU and the WebGPU tracer). `samplingPattern` and `importance` are off by default; `lightSelection` defaults to `"auto"`, which is `"tree"` for a scene with more than 8 local lights and `"uniform"` for the rest, so scenes with a handful of lights draw the same pixels as before:

- `samplingPattern: "blue-noise-first"` uses a 64 by 64 void-and-cluster tile (`src/path-tracer-blue-noise.js`, same bytes for the same seed) for the pixel jitter and the first two random numbers of sample 0, as `SAMPLING_PATTERN_BLUE_NOISE_FIRST` does in Cycles. Later samples are random.
- `lightSelection: "importance"` replaces the uniform light draw past the first hit: the sun and the sky keep their uniform share, and the local lights split theirs by power over squared distance, surface cosine and spot cone, mixed 10 percent with a uniform part. It ignores shadows and is a flat sum over the lights, not a tree (that is `lightSelection: "tree"`) nor ReSTIR; a scene with one local light or none draws the same pixels as before.
- `lightSelection: "tree"` is the light tree of Conty and Kulla (2018) as Cycles builds it (`src/kernel/light/tree.h`), in `src/path-tracer-light-tree.js`, the same code and table on the CPU reference and in the GLSL kernel. The point, spot and area lights are leaves of a binary tree built with the surface area orientation heuristic of the paper (12 buckets per axis, cost = energy times box area times orientation measure of both sides, as Cycles builds it). Each node keeps its box, an orientation cone (merged as in the paper) and the sum of its energy. A shading point walks down from the root and picks a child at each step by the importance of its box and cone: energy times the cosine of the best angle the box and cone allow, times the surface cosine, over the squared distance to the nearest point of the box. The step takes the chance 0.9 of the child's share of the maximum measure plus 0.1 of its share of a minimum measure (energy over the squared farthest distance, no angles; Cycles mixes them half and half), so a node the angle test culls keeps a chance and the image stays unbiased while little of the draw goes to lights that cannot reach the point; the chance of the whole path is divided out. The sun and the sky are the distant emitters and keep their uniform share of the emitter draw. It ignores shadows and spot blend. On a part with light linking that leaves some local light out, the tree is skipped and the draw is uniform among the lights the part is linked to (the tree weighs a node by every light under it, so it would spend draws on lights the part cannot see). With more than 16 local lights the first hit draws one light by the tree too, where every other selection traces a shadow ray to each light there. A scene with one local light or none draws the same pixels as the uniform choice.

## Real-time hybrid view (issue #624)

`67js/addons/path-tracer-realtime` (`createRealtimeTracer(scene, { width, height, bounces, ... })`, then `frame(camera)` for every picture) draws the WebGL2 tracer fast enough to move the camera. It shares the WebGL2 context of the GPU still, so one render at a time owns it, and it draws to `canvas` (an `OffscreenCanvas` or a canvas element, 8 bit, ACES view transform). `readFrame()` returns the same picture as bytes. The pieces, each in its own module so they can move to the WebGPU backend:

- **Half-size tracing.** One path per pixel and frame at half the output size (`resolutionDivisor`, 2), two bounces (`bounces`, 2). The first hit picks one light by importance instead of tracing a shadow ray to every light, which the denoiser averages over frames.
- **Radiance cache** (`src/path-tracer-radiance-cache.js`, SHaRC style). A cell is the quantized position (cells double in size each time the distance from the eye doubles past 64 cells) and the quantized normal of a surface point, hashed into 2^20 slots with no collision check (about 0.4% of 7,500 test cells collided). The last hit of a path reads its cell instead of tracing on; the hit before it writes what the rest of the path brought back, and a point pass adds the records into the cells with additive blending (WebGL2 has no atomics), as a running average of up to 128 records. The cache reads as diffuse. It needs the scene to rest: a new scene empties it.
- **Temporal denoiser and upsampler** (`src/path-tracer-temporal-denoise.js`, `src/path-tracer-reprojection.js`, SVGF style). The light is divided by the albedo, reprojected history (bilinear, rejected where distance or normal differ) is blended in over up to 48 frames, the variance comes from the history of the luminance (from the 3 by 3 neighbours while the history is short), four a-trous passes filter with plane distance, normal and variance guided luminance weights, and a joint bilateral upsample to the output size multiplies the albedo history back in. A sample far above its neighbours is clamped while the history is short.

What it is not (the issue asked for more): primary hits are traced, not taken from a raster G-buffer (the tracer's first hit already writes albedo, normal and distance; sharing the rasterizer's depth pass would save a ray per pixel and is left open), and the WebGL2 backend's motion vectors come from the camera's move only, so a moving part would ghost there (the WebGPU backend follows moved parts, below). Material ids are not used for history rejection on the WebGL2 backend (the WebGPU backend uses them, below). The upsample is a joint bilinear filter over half-size data with jittered rays averaged over time, not a full-size accumulation.

**WebGPU backend** (`createRealtimeTracer(scene, { backend: 'webgpu' })`, `src/path-tracer-realtime-webgpu.js`, `-kernel.js` and `-passes.js`). The same frame as WGSL compute in one command buffer. The megakernel of `src/path-tracer-webgpu.js` gets the cache pieces through `buildMegakernelSource({ realtime: true })`: the last hit reads the cache table, the hit before it writes a record, the first hit also picks one light by importance, and the entry point writes radiance with distance, albedo, normal and the material of the first hit to a frame buffer. The cache update is two dispatches: records are summed per cell with atomic adds on fixed point numbers (1/1024, a record clamped at 32), then every cell folds its sum into its running average (up to 128 records) and empties the sum. The denoiser (accumulate, four a-trous steps, upsample with tone mapping) is the same maths as the WebGL2 passes, with one addition: history is rejected where the material at the first hit differs, which stops the light of one surface from bleeding into a neighbour it passes behind. The result is packed into a buffer and copied to `canvas` (the tracer's own, or `options.canvas`). Without `navigator.gpu`, an adapter or 10 storage buffers per stage, `backend: 'webgpu'` falls back to WebGL2 and the tracer reports `backend` and `fallbackReason`. Differences to the WebGL2 object: `frame(camera, { wait: true })` returns `{ frame, done }` (await `done` to time a frame), `readFrame()` and `update()` return promises, and it does not share the WebGL2 context, so it can run beside a WebGL2 render. `bench/realtime-tracer/run.mjs --backend webgpu` measures it (one output directory per backend).

Measured on the RTX 3060 with `bench/realtime-tracer/run.mjs` (1920 by 1080 output, 960 by 540 traced, median of 200 frames, three runs of a fresh tracer, the card held by the job): `lit-interior` 20.0 to 20.1 ms orbiting and 20.1 to 20.5 ms resting; the demo room (`tests/fixtures/demo-room.recipe.json`) 20.2 ms orbiting 0.5 degrees per frame (it turns away from the heavy view) and 36.2 to 36.3 ms at the scene's own camera, 27.6 frames per second, 3 ms over the 33 ms target. With `bounces: 1` the demo room at its own camera took 24.5 ms (one run of 100 frames). On a shared card the same runs took up to 62 ms, so time it behind the GPU lock. FLIP of the frame after one second of rest (51 frames) against Cycles, `lit-interior`: 0.0637, against 0.0769 for the 64 sample still (the demo room has no Cycles render). `bench/realtime-tracer/score.py` scores it and `sheet.py` makes the contact sheet of a camera move: no trails behind the sphere, box or sign in either scene, but a fast move leaves visible speckle in dark parts of the demo room, where one sample per pixel and a cold cache are not enough.

WebGPU backend, second round (issue #624, `bench/realtime-tracer/run.mjs --backend webgpu`, same card, a loaded machine, so compare rows of one run only). Tried and not kept: a temporal history at the output size (each output pixel gathering the traced samples by their jittered positions) cost 6 to 7 ms on the demo room (21.4 to 27.7 ms at rest) and did not lower FLIP against Cycles on `lit-interior` (0.0579 against 0.0597 after 60 frames), so the remaining error is the lighting, not the resolution of the history. Moving parts: `update({ parts })` with the same part count keeps the history and cache, and a per-part motion table (`partMotionTable` in `src/path-tracer-reprojection.js`, indexed by the material id the kernel already writes) reprojects history on a part that moved or turned and rejects it on a part whose geometry changed. In the sliding box bench (`--mode ghost`) the trail left behind is the same with and without the table (1.69 levels in both), because the material id test already rejects the empty floor; what the table adds is history kept on the moving surface, which that bench does not measure. A scene update still rebuilds the BVH and the kernel (about 0.5 s), so this is not yet a per-frame animation path. Primary hits from a raster G-buffer are not done.

WebGPU backend, same setup (2026-11-09, `--backend webgpu`, median of 200 frames, three runs of a fresh tracer, card held through the GPU lock, awaiting `onSubmittedWorkDone` after every frame): `lit-interior` 10.6 to 11.0 ms orbiting and 12.1 to 12.2 ms resting (WebGL2 in the same session: 20.1 to 20.3 ms and 20.1 to 20.3 ms); the demo room 12.0 to 12.5 ms orbiting and 19.6 to 19.9 ms at the scene's own camera (WebGL2: 20.4 to 31.9 ms and 36.3 ms), so the 33 ms target holds with 13 ms to spare, at 1920 by 1080 output and 960 by 540 traced. The kernel compiles in 0.46 s against 10.8 s. FLIP of the frame after one second of rest against Cycles, `lit-interior`: 0.0504 (84 frames) against 0.0637 on WebGL2 (51 frames), the gain mostly from the frames that fit in the second. The contact sheet of a camera move on the demo room (8 frames, 2 degrees each) shows no trails; speckle in the dark floor is as on WebGL2. Left open: primary hits from a raster G-buffer, per-object motion vectors, and full-size accumulation instead of the joint bilinear upsample.

### Cold first draw (issue #861)

## Metal against Cycles (issue #959)

Two things made Cook's metal scene differ from Cycles outside the fireflies, and both are fixed in the shared WGSL of the native and the webgpu backend:

- Rough metal lost energy. Single scattering GGX drops the light that bounces between microfacets, 8 percent head on at roughness 0.5 and more at grazing angles. The specular lobe now carries the multiple scattering factor of Fdez-Aguera (2019) from a table of the lobe's white albedo (`specularWhiteAlbedo`, integrated over the hemisphere with this kernel's Smith masking), also applied to dielectric highlights.
- Sparse bright speckle on the floor near glossy metal came from diffuse, glossy, light paths. After a bounce Cook now widens the glossy lobes the way Cycles' `filter_glossy = 1` does: the smallest sampling density met on the path sets a floor of `0.5 * sqrt(1 - pdf)` for the lobe's alpha.
